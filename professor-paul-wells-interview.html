The Magic Behind Animation

In conversation with Professor Paul Wells

What makes drawings seem alive? Why do animated animals talk? How can a cartoon make us laugh, wonder, or even cry? Young contributors ask Professor Paul Wells about the art and imagination of animation.

This page presents the complete questions and responses supplied for the interview. A selection appears in our October magazine.

01When I draw a character on paper, it just sits there. How do animators make drawings come alive?

One way of thinking about an answer to this question is to think about an exercise animators at Pixar were asked to do. Simply, they were given some source photographs that the American painter and illustrator Norman Rockwell used for some of his work, and asked how Rockwell had moved from the realism of the photos to the ‘caricature’ of the images he created. This was done simply through exaggerating to some degree each aspect of a figure – eyes were wider, smiles were bigger, arms were extended, gestures made larger, bodies made plumper or thinner etc. This moved the characters beyond naturalism and made them more like a cartoon. The most important aspect of this approach though is that the characters must ‘do’ something, however small or big, to make an implied movement have an aspect of storytelling – winking, staring, waving, making a sandwich, pulling the curtains etc. So, the combination of exaggerated motion with movement that implies a narrative, again however small, ensures a sense of a character coming alive.

02Can a character look happy but actually be sad?

Yes. This is often revealed in two principle ways. The first is through the reactions of other characters, who perhaps question why someone might be so contented when the situation they seem to be in should make them sad. Other characters might discuss the happy character and question why they are so content, etc, making the viewer wonder if the character is just ‘putting on a face’ and hiding something. The other method is through the environment they are in – this might be a place which evidences poverty, or danger, or potential threat or jeopardy. By placing a character in a situation which does not seem to match their happiness the viewer starts to question if they really are happy or hiding their true feelings. This might be the case in Cinderella, perhaps, and we only see her truly happy when she goes to the ball and wins the heart of the Prince. Normally, happiness is expressed when a character has reached their goal or celebrates or overtly enjoys the company of others, while on many occasions ‘happiness’ is seen as a ‘performance’ that disguises other feelings. Sometimes sadness, but also deception, confusion, insecurity etc.

03Sometimes animated characters do things that are completely impossible, like falling from a cliff and walking away unharmed. Why do we accept these things in cartoons when we know they cannot happen in real life?

Simply, animation is always telling the viewer that it is NOT recorded reality but created reality as it might be depicted through drawing, puppets, clay, computer images, and lots of other materials. Animation is always telling us it is an illusion, and though we might ‘believe’ in the characters and their environment, it does not disguise that it has been made. This re-assures the audience because they know that all non-realistic actions and depictions are imaginative and fantastical at some level, and not naturalistic. This means the action can be plausible as comedy, magic, fantasy, thoughts, dreams, etc but is never realistic in an everyday life sort-of-way. Don’t forget animation can defy all the laws of the physical world and depict impossible action as its unique language, so it provides a different model of creativity for animators, practitioners and writers.

04If you showed us a cartoon without any words or music, could we still understand the whole story?

Often animators have this very ambition in their minds when they start to work. If a story can be created purely visually it can be seen all around the world and make sense to anyone whatever their language. Music always helps, of course. Often animators say that they would like their animation to be like music and prompt understanding and emotion not through sound, but color, shape, form and the use of space and time. Combined, the two languages of image and sound connect with viewers in many different ways, and this can range from experimental films right through to visual storytelling that sometimes resembles the action, pantomime, gesture and performance in live action silent films. As human beings we engage with life through all the senses – animation is very good at directing viewers to look at specific things and make sense of them through either their literal meaning (ie I am drinking something) and / or their associations (ie I might be drinking poison, or medicine, or alcohol etc). It is the same with sound. So, animation nearly always operates at a straightforward level of understanding but also uses symbols and metaphor a great deal.

05Why do so many cartoon animals talk, wear clothes, and behave like humans?

This was a question I asked myself many years ago when I wrote a book called ‘The Animated Bestiary’. Why do so many animated films from all over the world, for over a century of production, feature animals ? There are many answers to this question but the main one is that it is often much easier for human beings to express ideas about what it is to be human by not representing themselves as human. By being ‘an animal’ this often gets around issues of race, religion, identity, or even familiarity etc, and allows animators either to construct their characters with some degree of ‘innocence’ or the very opposite – they can smuggle through ideas that might be opposed, or not permitted in certain countries, or simply which are complex but which can be expressed symbolically or as a metaphor when represented as an animal. Animals have a long history of meanings in fables and fairytales so animation often draws on that too. The expression of human characteristics in animals, objects, environments etc is called anthropomorphism, and though some people criticize its use, it really does help in both being appealing to viewers (particularly children) but also representing issues and ideas. Have a look at my book if you can – your teacher might be able to use some of it to help you understand these ideas more.

06In Zootopia, Judy Hopps is a rabbit who wants to become a police officer, even though everyone thinks rabbits are too small for the job. Why do you think the filmmakers chose a rabbit to tell this story?

Rabbits are very appealing characters, and are often domestic pets, so this helps to create more appeal in the character and so that a viewer might sympathize with them more easily. They will especially like the idea that a ‘small’ creature can be successful against all the other bigger animals. There is a long history of great animated rabbits like Oswald, Bugs Bunny and Peter Rabbit, so all of this helps children and adults identify with the character and support them in what they do. In a more realistic animation like ‘Watership Down’, the rabbits are not treated well, so our feelings for them as animals and pets is even stronger. Sometimes the choice of the animal is about its associations with other things from other stories, or even because the animator had a favorite pet !

07Mickey Mouse and Pluto are both animals, but Mickey talks and behaves like a person while Pluto behaves like an ordinary dog. Why are they so different when they live in the same cartoon world?

This kind of character construction is about the kind of relationships the writers and animators want the audience to have with the characters. Seeing relationships as ‘adult and pet’, ‘close friends’, ‘sweethearts’, ‘hero/heroine and villain’, ‘strong and weak’, ‘big and small’, ‘talkative and quiet’ etc is an important and immediate way in which the audience understands the roles each character plays so that the story can proceed quickly and with everyone fully understanding each character’s aims, goals, and needs. The ‘cartoon world’ allows each to ‘behave’ in more extreme ways which exaggerate their key characteristics – Donald Duck, for example, is all about starting calm, getting frustrated, and then releasing his anger, but in a comic, cartoon way. He mainly ends up looking a bit silly or foolish, but never harmed or criticized. Sometimes the real characteristics associated with the animals help us to understand the relationships, but not always. Often the idea that one animal might actually eat another is used as a key part of a story, but sometimes this is ignored altogether.

08If we replaced Simba in The Lion King with a human child, would we feel the same way about his story? What do you think?

This is a good question but is probably answered by watching The Jungle Book, which features Mowgli, a little boy, both protected by animals, and threatened by others. This is the same for Simba, but I do not think it differs in the sense, that we see Simba ’s place more clearly in the ‘circle of life’ and in the hierarchy of a family. He is going to be King one day, but we have to see how he grows up as a child and becomes a young man because of the things that happen to him with his own species, and the broader threat of nature. We also see him having a romantic relationship too, and that is an important part of growing up. All these things mean that Simba is a part of the animal kingdom, while Mowgli is encouraged to leave the animal kingdom and re-join humankind.

09In Cinderella, a pumpkin becomes a carriage and mice become horses. If you were animating this scene, how would you decide what the transformation should look like?

In many ways because in animation, whatever you might imagine, you can probably create these transitions could be conducted in any way you would like. Normally though you would look to change one ‘shape’ into another, expanding the pumpkin, for example into things that are broadly ‘circular’ and may resemble other objects before finally becoming a coach. Similarly the mice might expand into bigger four-legged creatures before finally becoming a horse. In animation this is called ‘metamorphosis’ and is one of the great things about animation because one thing can literally transform into another by the rapid shift of lines, or clay, or computer shapes etc. Think of the genie in Aladdin when it explains all the things he could be – this is a perfect example of metamorphosis.

10In Beauty and the Beast, objects like a teapot, a clock, and a candlestick can talk and have feelings. How do animators decide what an object would look like if it suddenly came alive?

Again, they probably think of a person, or take reference from the performances of the actors, perhaps thinking of a tall man for the candlestick, a cuddly grandma for a teapot, etc. They also look at the object to see the things that look like faces – eyes are very important – or look like limbs. The more the object can resemble something that is human, then the more it comes alive and the audience can identify with them. Human beings naturally do this anyway, often seeing faces in objects and environments, and finding human movement in things like trees or fields of corn. Objects also have a lots of associations with what they are actually used for, so animators also try and use these actions to extend their familiarity with human behavior and the ways humans do things.

11In Frozen, Elsa creates an entire ice palace while singing. How do animators make a scene feel so magical that we almost forget it is impossible?

Human beings love spectacle and performances that seem larger than life. The more something makes you go ‘wow, how did they do that ?’, the more you have a feeling of wonder that takes your feelings beyond those you might experience in everyday life to a place that you rarely experience so it becomes magical and memorable. Some people call this feeling ‘the sublime’, The excess and seeming impossibility of what you are seeing carries you along because in animation IT IS POSSIBLE !! That’s what makes the form so special and so significant because it can transport people into situations and spectacular places they never experience. (Don’t forget all the Marvel movies, Star Wars etc have lots of animation that makes this happen, it’s just more realistic animation than Pixar or Disney style cartoons).

12When Disney turns an old fairy tale into an animated movie, it sometimes changes the characters, the ending, or even the meaning of the story. Who decides what should stay and what can change?

This is the job of the screenwriter who adapts the original story for the audiences it is being made for. Fairytales are often dark, full of complex imagery, and violent or unhappy endings, so this is often modified to be less frightening or challenging for viewers. More often it is about telling a clear story that everyone will understand and uses animation for the best possible purposes, perhaps using metamorphosis as mentioned above. Producers and Directors will also have a view of course because they are looking to make a certain kind of film, so the familiar fairytale elements will be very important, but other things may be invented. Disney created the seven dwarfs as separate characters – they were just described as ‘dwarfs’ in the original story, so this gave them more storytelling and musical opportunities accordingly. Fairytales have lots of things that can be done well in animation – magic, transformations, unusual characters, impossible action etc, so ensuring that there is a clear story structure is important because there will inevitably be lots of non-naturalistic / non-realist scenes.

13Tom and Jerry hardly ever need to speak, but they can make us laugh so much. How can a cartoon be funny without using words?

This is normally about the dramatic or comic action – Tom and Jerry use lots of comic violence; the Roadrunner uses endless chases; Bugs Bunny uses lots of verbal wit; all TEX AVERY cartoons use mad, surreal visual gags etc. But even going back to Felix the Cat he uses visual puns where one object looks like another and enables the animator to create a visual joke. My book Understanding Animation has lots on comedy in animation which your teacher might be able to help you with. Also Frank Thomas and Ollie Johnson, two of the original Disney animators in the book ‘The Illusion of Life’ explain all of Disney’s approach to visual jokes.

14In Inside Out, Joy and Sadness are feelings, but they look and behave like people. How can animators turn something invisible, like a feeling, into a character we can actually see?

This is one of the most special characteristics of animation. The animator can give form and character to an invisible concept, or simply something we can’t see, like an interior of something, or even a state of mind. Animation is great at dreams, memories, thoughts and fantasies in the mind. It is brilliant at showing us how something works - think of all those shampoo adverts where we see shampoo ‘bubbles’ cleaning hair follicles. And it is especially good at taking a concept (joy, anger etc) or an element (water, wind etc) and giving it a personality by virtue of two things. Its associations (Sad is blue, slow because it’s depressed, philosophic because it’s resigned, etc) and its established color and movement codes (Anger is red, connected with fire raging, suggestive of blood coursing, etc). Once the associations and motion possibilities are considered it is comparatively straightforward to develop the character because the design and movements are established.

15Sometimes a cartoon character does something naughty or selfish, but we still like them. How do animators make us care about characters who aren't always good?

Whether we like to admit it or not, we are often quite attracted to ‘villains’ because they are mischievous and break the rules. This is what many of us wish we could do at times, and either don’t dare to, or fear we may be caught and / or punished. ‘Naughty’ characters help to build what is called ‘dramatic conflict’ - they create the problems or issues ‘the hero / heroine’ has to solve. Sometimes they go too far - too violent, too unjust, too cruel to characters we like, for example - and we cease to like them and want them defeated. While their actions are playful, mischievous, and naughty we quite admire their ability to see things differently, and not to conform. Think of the ‘class clown’ (which I was at school) - others often like them even though they are very disruptive while being funny. ‘Disruption’ of the normal and orthodox is necessary in most dramas and comedies so these kinds of characters are really significant.

16When a character in an animated movie dies or loses someone they love, we sometimes cry—even though we know the character is just a drawing. Why can cartoons make us feel such real sadness?

Cartoons which include serious and emotional scenes try to encourage what is known as ‘empathy’ - the ability to share the feelings of the characters they are watching. Normally this is about trying to prompt similar situations that people may have experienced and emotions they have felt. Many of us have lost a pet, a relative, a friend, etc and we feel grief, sometimes never quite getting over the loss. These are the kinds of feelings animation tries to tap into - Disney has many situations, for example, where a child loses a parent eg Bambi, The Lion King, Finding Nemo, or misses something badly, for example, Riley in Pixar’s Inside Out, who misses her former home so much she experiences depression and near breakdown. These are very adult themes which both children and their parents can negotiate together. Pixar’s Coco has the very big theme of ‘you only exist until the last person who remembers you dies’ so like Soul, explores what memory is, and even creates the afterlife to discuss these ideas. Animation really can accommodate all things because it can create the imaginary contexts and situations to look at challenging ideas in an accessible way.

17You have worked with people who create animated stories. Have you ever seen an idea that sounded completely silly at first but turned into something wonderful?

I was in Japan working on an exhibition of the British studio Halas & Batchelor, which was being staged at the Studio Ghibli Gallery. It was at the time when the studio was developing Spirited Away. The writer and Director Hayao Miyazaki said he wanted to make a film about a bored and slightly spoilt young girl who was moving to a new home with her parents. To be honest no one felt excited about this description. Trust Miyazaki though to put Chihiro, the little girl, into a surreal and supernatural world full of spirits, echoing many stories in Japanese folklore, as she tries to rescue her parents, who have turned into pigs ! The film addresses many themes including the environment, consumerism and tradition. It is a masterpiece of animation. From little acorns, great oaks grow.

18If two animators were given exactly the same story, could they make two completely different movies? What would make their versions different?

Yes. We have often set this exercise for students giving them the same script to develop, storyboard and make a sequence from. It all becomes about how you interpret the characters (this can seriously affect their design and attitude, for example) and the situations / context they are involved in. Environments can be imagined in a very different way from the abstract to the highly naturalistic. The Director might want to stress a dominant theme; actors may adopt different accents and outlooks that position them in the narrative in a different way. Any script is just a blueprint but the language of animation is so extensive that the same script can produce many different films. This does require that you really know and exploit the vocabulary available in animation, otherwise you may as well shoot in live action.

19Nowadays, computers can help create incredibly realistic animated characters. But do you think a simple hand-drawn cartoon can sometimes tell a story better than a very realistic one?

Yes. This is especially clear when you go to animation festivals all around the world. There are so many different styles, approaches, techniques and models of storytelling and expression. I love the fact that nations everywhere make animation and use it to really express ideas and experience in a variety of ways that reflect the personalities and outlooks of different people. The hand drawn is just one choice - Asian cultures use calligraphy and watercolor designs; Eastern Europe loves its puppets and clay animation; the US specializes in 3D but it is used in Western Europe in imaginative, less expensive, hyperrealistic ways. The most important thing though is a good idea or concept, or a great story or comic narrative, since if applied in the right style with the appropriate technique, each can be as affecting and successful as another.

20When you were little, was there a cartoon character or animated movie that made you think, "How did they do that?" Do you still feel that sense of wonder when you watch animation today?

I remember vividly puppet animations like Bagpuss and Pogles Wood in the UK because they moved but had no obvious strings like The Woodentops, or later, Thunderbirds. In my own play I realized that you could make certain toys in particular ways to make them stand up or sit down, or make arm gestures, but I was baffled about how they might come to life and move on their own. I wondered about cartoons as well - British ones like Mr Benn or Captain Pugwash, or Mole, which I later found out was from what is now Czechia, and even Tom & Jerry, or Bugs. What even is that ? How is it done ? Then I read ‘an interview’ with Bugs Bunny in Film Review when I was a young boy. It implied people drew him and made him move. My mum then took me to the library and I found a book on making animated films. I read it from cover to cover and finally found out how Bagpuss, Bugs and a host of other characters moved. Amazing ! And frankly, I still remain amazed to this very day !

21If a cartoon character is inspired by someone or something, can they later be changed into something unrealistic/magical to adapt to the story lines of the series/movie?

Yes, but viewers like animated characters to largely remain the same unless they are a developing character in a series. This is normally in more adult work like Bojack Horseman, for example. Let’s take an example though. Tom & Jerry had three main phases, although they continue today, but first they were made by Hanna Barbera at MGM, second for a short time by Gene Deitch in what was the Czech Republic, and last by the master animator, Chuck Jones at Warner Bros. The first period included the classic slapstick violence and love-hate relationship of the two characters; the second phase used the characters in odd situations where the humor was about Eastern European weirdness; and the third phase made the characters highly sentimental and gentle with no violent chases at all ! The first shorts are, of course, the best, and remain so today ! Any character can be adapted and developed, but usually, especially if the character is popular, audiences want the character to stay the same.

22What is the difference between cartoons and anime? Which do you like more, and why?

In terms of technique cartoons and anime are very similar but the answer is essentially cultural. ‘Cartoons’ emerged most famously in the USA, though they were made elsewhere, and ‘Anime’ comes from Japan where many kinds of animation are made for both children and adults, that often have their design and narrative sources in Manga. This is a broad distinction and there are now many crossovers embracing many different visual sources and stories. I love both of course because I think Tex Avery cartoons are still the funniest and most inventive ever made and I loved things like Akira and Ghost in the Shell because they are more sci-fi and adult. I value the different kinds of art and creative skill in both.

23When I read a book, I often imagine what each character might look like. This may be different from what the author might have imagined. How do animators create the looks of beloved characters to make sure it matches how majority of the people have imagined them?

The animator can undertake research in a number of ways. How has the character been treated in art and illustration before ? What are the characteristics of the character and how might they be developed ? Does the character have to look a certain way to reflect a certain studio style (Disney or Ghibli or Aardman, for example) or iconic representations that author copyright insists upon (Beatrix Potter). Does the character have to incorporate national or indigenous design styles familiar to its home audiences first ? The animator could also talk to children and adults about how they imagine the character and perhaps ask them to draw it if they can. When all this is done it is still up to the imaginative animator to design an appealing and attractive character that might draw on all this information but it still needs to be unique even if familiar.

24What would you suggest to a child you want to be an animator when they grow up?

My suggestion would be to draw, draw and draw some more; make as many 3D things as you can; watch as many different styles of animation as possible, and maybe imitate other design styles in your own drawing; read about how animation and film is made. Use your phone to make simple animations of objects moving and slowly but surely develop stories. Maybe use buttons and coins in stop motion just to get the thrill of making something move. Watch people carefully to see how they move and express themselves. Think about color and shape in all the ways it is used. Go to galleries and read comics and graphic novels. Basically be curious about everything and take every opportunity to create. An overall understanding of all the arts is good, too. Eventually an animator magically emerges !
